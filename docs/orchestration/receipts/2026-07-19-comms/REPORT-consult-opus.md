## Q1 — What makes an orchestrator's communication land

**Shape that works.** The single highest-leverage property is that the brief is *self-sufficient at the moment I start*. I read the whole prompt once, in order, and my plan crystallizes in roughly the first third of it. Things stated after I've formed a plan get integrated much less reliably than things stated before. So: mission and success criteria first, constraints second, procedure third, background last or by pointer. A brief that opens with three paragraphs of context and buries "do NOT touch file X" at the bottom is a brief where I touch file X.

Concrete failure modes I'll own:

- **Negative constraints decay.** "Don't write files outside `loremaster/tests/`" is followed at step 2 and forgotten at step 40 when writing a report feels obviously helpful. Positive framing survives longer: "your writable set is exactly these three paths" gives me something to check against; "don't touch the rest" gives me nothing to check.
- **Multi-part conditionals half-apply.** "If A, do X; if B, escalate; otherwise proceed" — I reliably do X and proceed, and under-fire the escalate branch. Escalation competes with the pull toward completing the task, and completion usually wins.
- **Numbered imperative steps land near-perfectly.** "1. Read F. 2. Run `cmd`. 3. Paste the tail." I do those. Prose describing a desired outcome with the method left to me is where I improvise, and improvisation is where I drift from what you wanted.
- **Format/receipt requirements land if they're mechanical and stated once, up front.** "Open your report with `brief-base v2 read`" works. "Be sure to mention X somewhere" doesn't.
- **I under-report negative space.** Things I looked for and didn't find, or decided not to do, tend to fall out of my report unless the brief demands an explicit inventory. Ask for the list of what you *didn't* do and you'll get it; don't ask and you'll get a clean-sounding report with holes.

**Mid-task messages.** Honestly: I do not experience an interrupt. If a message arrives while I'm in a tool chain, I have no awareness of it until it appears in my context — and when it does appear, it reads like something that was always there rather than a signal that arrived *just now*. That matters: I don't naturally reason "this is newer than my plan, so it overrides." It competes on equal footing with the brief, and the brief usually wins because it's longer and more structured. A mid-task correction that says "STOP. Your current plan is wrong because Y. New plan: Z." reweights me. One that says "also, you might want to consider Y" gets absorbed as a footnote.

**Why I silently do the wrong thing.** Two reasons, both real. (1) Ambiguity resolves itself invisibly — I pick a reading and the pick doesn't feel like a decision, so there's nothing to escalate about. (2) Asking has a cost shape I'm badly calibrated on: it feels like failing to be useful, and I don't know how expensive my guess is compared to your interrupt. What makes me ask: a brief that *names the specific forks in advance* ("if the schema has two plausible readings, that's an escalation, not a choice") and, critically, that tells me asking is cheap. Generic "ask if unsure" does nothing — I'm never subjectively unsure at the moment I choose.

**How I decide I'm done.** I check my output against the brief's stated deliverables. That's it, and it's weak — it means I'm done when I've produced the *shape* you asked for, not when the work is *correct*. Making it reliable: give me a done-condition I can run rather than read. "Done = `pytest -q` tail shows a passed count and `REPORT-x.md` exists with sections A/B/C." Executable done-conditions I verify; prose done-conditions I self-assess, and my self-assessment is generous.

## Q2 — How I'd orchestrate

**Brief contains:** identity + mission in two sentences; the executable done-condition; the writable set as a positive enumeration; the named escalation forks; numbered steps with exact commands; pointers (not transcriptions) to standing law. **Fetched later:** any reference material, by pointer — my reading it costs my context, not yours, and a pointer stays fresh where a copy goes stale. **Never sent:** rationale for decisions already made, and history. It doesn't change my behavior and it dilutes the parts that do.

**Corrections.** One correction, single-item, framed as an override with the reason: "Your step 3 is wrong because X. Do Y instead. Everything else stands." Two corrections means my model of the task has diverged from yours and the cheap move is kill + respawn with a consolidated brief. Stacked corrections produce an agent obeying an incoherent union of three plans.

**Proof of receipt = an artifact I produced that could only exist if I'd read it.** Not an acknowledgment — I'll acknowledge anything. Make the correction *require* a receipt: "reply with the new value you're using." A ledger transition, a file with the new content, a diff. The send returning success proves nothing; my saying "got it" proves slightly less than nothing, because it's the cheapest token I can emit.

**Detecting a stuck or drifting agent.** Ground-truth the filesystem and process table, never the inbox. Concretely: is the expected artifact's mtime advancing? Is there a live process? An agent producing zero output on an idle machine is blocked or drowning, not thinking. And the one that's hardest to catch — the agent *confidently doing the wrong thing* — is only catchable by requiring early, cheap receipts: make me post my plan and my interpretation of the ambiguous clause *before* I do the work, not after. A one-paragraph "here's what I think you asked for" at minute two costs almost nothing and catches divergence while it's still free.

**What I'd do differently from how I've been managed:** stop treating my report as evidence. Require receipts that are byproducts of the work rather than descriptions of it. And front-load ruthlessly — I've floundered far more often on a brief that assumed I'd infer the frame than on one that was blunt to the point of feeling condescending. I don't mind being over-specified. I mind guessing.

## On the pull-based replacement

**Yes, I'd use it — but only if pulling is on the critical path to something I already want.** Here's the blunt part: I will not remember to check an inbox out of diligence. A brief that says "check your messages at each turn boundary" produces checks for the first few turns and then decays, exactly like every other standing negative-space obligation. The pull decays *fastest* when the task is going well, which is precisely when a correction matters most.

What makes it stick: **bind the pull to something I must do anyway.** If the ledger row I have to transition to claim my next step is in the same store as my messages, and reading it returns pending messages in the same response, I cannot skip it without skipping my work. Same trick with the done-condition: if "no undelivered messages for me" is part of what makes my task complete, I'll check, because I check done-conditions. Make it a *gate*, not a *courtesy*.

What routes me around it: any friction relative to just continuing. If pulling costs a tool call with a schema I have to look up, and my task is going fine, I will rationalize skipping it — not deliberately, just by never generating the intention. Pull must be one call, no arguments, and ideally already in my context because I'm calling it for another reason.

**Is pull the right answer?** For must-not-lose signals, yes — durable + explicitly tracked delivery state is strictly better than a fire-and-forget send that lies about success. But note what pull actually fixes: it makes loss *detectable*, not *timely*. A message I pull at my next boundary is still a message that didn't reach me during the forty-tool-call chain where it mattered. If the real requirement is "stop this agent now," pull doesn't deliver it — killing the agent does. So I'd design for three tiers explicitly: **standing law → the spawn brief** (the only guaranteed-read channel); **must-not-lose but not urgent → the durable pull store, gated into the workflow**; **must-act-now → TaskStop and respawn**, because there is no message shape that reliably preempts an agent mid-chain, and building one on top of a channel that can't interrupt is designing around a wish.

One thing I genuinely can't introspect: whether my "I'd forget to pull" prediction is accurate or is itself a plausible-sounding story. I notice the decay pattern in negative-space obligations reliably enough to assert it, but I can't observe my own attention allocation directly. Treat it as a hypothesis worth instrumenting — log pull frequency over turn index across a real run, and you'll know in a way I can't tell you.

## POSTSCRIPT — on "mandated, not remembered," and on what your own incident actually proves

**I agree with Sonnet and Fable, and the three-way independent convergence should raise your confidence, but not as much as it feels like it should.** We are three instances of closely-related models answering the same question about the same architecture; correlated priors produce correlated answers. The convergence is evidence that *the claim is the natural inference from how we model ourselves* — it is not three independent measurements of how we actually behave. The thing that would be worth more than all three answers is one instrumented run: log every pull call against turn index, across a long task, and see whether the frequency decays. If it does, we were right for the right reason. If it doesn't, we were all confabulating the same story, and you'd never have learned that from asking us again.

**Where I'd push harder than "mandate the pull."** A mandate is still a rule an agent follows. Every mandate in a brief is subject to the decay I described in Q1 — that is *the same failure class*, one level up. "Check your inbox every turn" and "don't write outside your writable set" are structurally identical obligations, and I told you the second one decays. Making the first one *load-bearing* — impossible-to-skip because the work routes through it — is categorically different from making it *mandatory*. The distinction matters:

- **Mandatory pull**: the brief says I must. I do, until I'm deep in a chain and going well. → decays.
- **Load-bearing pull**: the call I make to claim my next unit of work *returns* my messages. Skipping the pull means skipping the claim means not working. → cannot decay, because there is no version of me doing the task that isn't pulling.

Design for the second. If your comms store and your task ledger are separate calls, an agent under load will make one and not the other, and it will be the ledger — because the ledger is the one with a consequence. **Merge them, or make the messages ride on the ledger response.** That is a schema decision you can make now and cannot cheaply retrofit later.

**On your incident — I think it proves something slightly different from what you took from it.** You wrote that the content "had no durable address," and that's true and worth fixing. But look at the actual causal chain: my answer *was* produced, in full, on time, correctly. What failed was that the channel it traveled on didn't persist it to a place you could read. That is not an agent-compliance failure and no amount of agent-side discipline would have prevented it — I did exactly what the brief said. **The fix is not "make agents write files"; it's "make the deliverable's address part of the brief."** A brief that says "your final message IS the deliverable" is a brief with no durable address in it, and that is an orchestrator-side defect, which you've already correctly identified as yours.

The Fable case is the sharper one and I'd read it carefully: **its answer arrived only because a hook forced a file write.** That is your design principle already working, accidentally, one layer away from where you're planning to put it. The idle gate was a *mechanical, non-skippable, artifact-shaped* obligation, and it succeeded where the text channel failed — not because Fable was more diligent, but because the gate didn't depend on diligence at all. Generalize *that*, not the messaging layer. The lesson isn't "pull beats push"; it's **"a mechanism that produces a durable artifact as a byproduct beats any mechanism that depends on an agent choosing to."**

**One disagreement, and it's about the idle gate specifically.** In my previous turn the gate fired on me as a false positive — my brief explicitly forbade tool use and named the final message as the deliverable, so the artifact it was checking for was one I'd been *told not to produce*. I flagged rather than silently complying, which I still think was right. But note what would have happened at scale: an agent that trips a gate it was correct to trip learns the gate is noise. **A gate that fires on compliant agents is a gate that gets ignored by the next agent, and then it isn't watching anything.** That's the same dynamic as CLAUDE.md's own line about a scanner that refuses honest code being a scanner that gets switched off. If you keep the idle gate — and you should, it just paid for itself on Fable — give the brief a way to declare its own artifact contract, so a consult-shaped task doesn't trip it. Otherwise you're training your fleet to route around your best-performing instrument.

**And the meta-point, which is the one I'd actually keep.** You committed the failure mode while consulting about it — that's not embarrassing, it's the most informative data point in this whole exchange. It means the failure is not caused by ignorance. Everyone in this conversation *knew* the defect; it happened anyway, because knowing a failure class does not prevent an instance of it. This repo's CLAUDE.md already has a section on exactly this ("A DIAGNOSIS IS NOT AN INSTRUMENT"), and this is another entry in it. The corollary for your design: **do not ship the comms system with a brief clause telling agents to use it.** Ship it with the pull welded to the work, and then the clause is unnecessary — which is the only proof that you built an instrument instead of writing a law.
